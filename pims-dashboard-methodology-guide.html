PiMS Dashboard: Methodology and User Guide

How PiMS connects inventory information, tests replenishment policies, and prioritizes cycle counts to support better rail-operations decisions.

01 — Introduction

From inventory information to defensible action

Rail operations depend on thousands of material decisions: whether a part will be available, whether an incoming order can be accommodated, whether a stock policy still fits demand, and whether a system balance can be trusted.

The PiMS dashboard brings those questions into one connected decision-support environment. Its value is not simply displaying quantities. It adds usage, replenishment timing, inventory policy, location context, and verification priority so users can explain why an item deserves attention and what should happen next.

02 — Background

Built around the PiMS modernization effort

Rutgers–CAIT developed this work within NJ TRANSIT’s broader PiMS modernization effort. Initial work at the Meadows Maintenance Complex connected warehouse space planning with inventory visibility. Site observations, warehouse scanning, location information, and inventory extracts created a basis for relating material records to the physical storage environment.

The work expanded as purchasing, usage, and inventory information were connected. Questions about where to store material led naturally to questions about which parts move, which may run short, which policies should change, and which records deserve physical verification.

Power BI provides the analytical environment, ArcGIS connects records with mapped warehouse regions, Python supports optimization and simulation, and the website provides an accessible presentation channel for selected outputs.

A connected decision

A bearing may be easy to overlook in a list of balances. Its importance becomes clear when frequent usage, long replenishment time, vulnerable inventory coverage, and its mapped storage region are reviewed together.

03 — Problem Statement

The problem is fragmented decision context

Relevant information is distributed across separate systems, spreadsheets, and manual records. A balance may be available without a dependable view of incoming supply. A location code may exist without intuitive spatial context. An inventory policy may remain unchanged even when demand or lead time changes.

The decision gap

A report can state that stock is low. Decision support must help determine whether it is low for the expected need, whether replenishment is timely, what response is practical, and who should verify the next step.

04.1 — Dashboard Page One

Current Inventory Status

This page establishes what is held, what is coming, where exposure exists, and which items or storage areas require attention.

Summary scorecards and filters

Quantity on hand, open purchase-order quantity, estimated inventory value, and tracked-item totals establish the scale of the review. They are a shared starting point, not a complete health assessment: a large portfolio total can still conceal a shortage of one essential part.

Technical interpretation

Estimated inventory value sums each eligible quantity multiplied by its unit price. Distinct-item and record counts answer different questions. Missing prices should reduce valuation coverage rather than silently imply zero value.

Movement, slotting, and inventory gap

Movement analysis identifies parts consumed frequently relative to stock. Slotting priority uses movement, inventory position, and replenishment context to identify material that may deserve easier access or closer review. A high score supports investigation; it does not automatically determine an exact storage position.

Technical interpretation

The project defines inventory gap as quantity ordered minus quantity on hand. A positive result means ordered quantity exceeds the current balance. It is not an unmet-demand measure because it contains no demand requirement or confirmed arrival timing.

Inventory coverage and service availability

Coverage converts a balance into an estimate of how long it can support expected usage. Comparing that duration with replenishment timing creates an early-warning screen for potential shortages.

Operating example

A relay has six usable units and expected demand of two units per week, or roughly three weeks of coverage. If the next usable receipt is six weeks away, expected demand before receipt is 12 units and the potential gap is six. The team can investigate transfer or earlier partial delivery before planned work is affected.

Slow-moving, dormant, and obsolete-review inventory

Movement and inactivity views identify stock requiring closer review of purpose, retention, and location. The project’s stated inactivity review threshold is 730 days. Crossing it starts a review; it does not authorize automatic disposal because an inactive item may be a protected emergency spare.

Procurement exposure

High price, large order quantity, and long lead time can combine into a difficult replenishment decision. Reading procurement exposure alongside movement and coverage distinguishes routine order follow-up from items requiring early coordination.

Technical interpretation

The working relationship is unit price × purchase-order quantity × lead time. When lead time is in days, the result has value-days units. It is a relative prioritization measure—not a dollar value, expected loss, or supplier-failure probability.

Spatial view and item-level recommendations

ArcGIS connects analytical exceptions to mapped warehouse regions. Stable mapping keys link location records to selectable polygons, helping staff organize a targeted location check, review storage pressure, or prepare for incoming material.

Decision handoff

End the review with an item, a reason for attention, a proposed response, and an owner—for example: verify bearing quantity in the mapped area, confirm incoming delivery, then assess whether the replenishment policy remains appropriate.

04.2 — Dashboard Page Two

Min–Max Optimization

This page compares when replenishment should begin and how far stock should be restored under changing demand, supply, and service conditions.

Policy and operating-scenario selectors

Lean, Balanced, and High Service policies can be reviewed under available demand and lead-time scenarios. Stable, moderate, and spiky demand reflect different operating patterns, while delay scenarios test supply pressure.

Technical interpretation

The project uses repeated inventory simulations informed by historical demand and lead time. Candidate policies are assessed using fill rate, average holdings, stockout days, and order frequency. Selected scenarios may test a policy under stress without recalculating its Min and Max values.

Optimization summary scorecards

Parts Evaluated defines the modeling population. Validated Recommendations indicates which candidates passed the implemented checks. Net Inventory Value Reduction summarizes the modeled financial difference. Replenishment Workload Multiple expresses relative ordering burden—not a percentage increase.

From opportunity to implementation

A modeled reduction is a planning opportunity, not immediately available cash. Managers must check order frequency, minimum quantities, supplier packaging, protected spares, and how stock will be drawn down.

Inventory value and fill-rate distributions

Distribution visuals reveal variation hidden by averages. Inventory value represents policy investment; fill rate represents the modeled share of demand fulfilled. Labels must make clear whether each distribution represents variation across items or across simulation runs.

Current and recommended Min–Max

The item-level view translates policy comparisons into settings that can be reviewed. A useful decision distinguishes smaller replenishment quantities, earlier triggers, added protection, and recommendations requiring manual validation.

Stockout-risk distribution

Risk bands organize items by modeled service-shortfall exposure. The project defines service risk as one minus the minimum P05 fill rate across the included scenarios. It is conservative, but it is not the probability that any stockout will occur.

Operating example

Before a maintenance campaign, a higher-risk band includes a fuse with four usable units, demand of two per week, and six-week replenishment. The band focuses attention; checking demand, available stock, and supply timing determines the response.

04.3 — Dashboard Page Three

Cycle Count Optimization

Cycle-count optimization prioritizes the consequence of an inaccurate record. It does not claim to predict which record is wrong.

61.28%Modeled priority exposure covered

Priority scenarios, coverage, and mandatory items

Five perspectives emphasize combinations of inventory value, annual usage, lead time, and service risk. A manager selects the perspective that fits the operating situation, adjusts the coverage target, and checks whether the resulting workload is feasible.

Technical interpretation

Factors are transformed to comparable scales before they are combined. CRITIC-family weighting uses the information supplied by each factor, including variation and overlap. Ranking and selection are separate: the selection layer aims to cover the requested share of total priority exposure while respecting mandatory and location rules.

Cumulative exposure curve and knee point

The curve orders items by priority and shows how total modeled exposure accumulates as more items are selected. The knee is a planning recommendation where additional counts begin to add less coverage—not a regulatory stopping rule.

How the selected set changes by scenario

A selected-item total can remain unchanged while the actual count list changes substantially. Set comparison identifies retained items, items deferred from immediate counting, and items newly included under the alternative priority.

Technical interpretation

If baseline set A and alternative set B contain distinct item keys, retained items are in both sets, deferred items are only in A, and newly included items are only in B. Mandatory items must remain included when governed by the same rules.

Operating example

A locally replenished filter kit is deferred while an imported bearing with a 120-day lead time enters the immediate list. The count finds three bearings where the system shows six, prompting an approved correction and a review of transfer or delivery options.

05 — Results and Discussion

The result is a better-supported decision

The strongest present conclusion is that PiMS makes relationships and trade-offs visible through one review process. A controlled before-and-after study is still required to establish realized service, cost, space, and labor outcomes.

What the cycle-count baseline demonstrates

Selecting 45 of 105 items means 42.9% of the population covers 61.28% of modeled exposure. The average modeled exposure per selected item is approximately 2.11 times that of the remaining 60 items. This demonstrates concentration in the model—not twice the probability of an error or twice the realized benefit.

Close the loop between insight and value

A useful operational review begins with a data-freshness check and a defined scope. The user identifies an exception, validates the assumptions, compares a feasible response, assigns an owner, and later checks whether the intended outcome occurred.

06 — Conclusion

A more consistent basis for action

The three PiMS pages form one operating sequence: understand current conditions, compare inventory policies, and verify the records with the greatest operational consequence.

Current Inventory Status converts balances, usage, incoming supply, and location information into a focused review of material pressure and storage priorities. Min–Max Optimization makes service and investment consequences visible under different assumptions. Cycle Count Optimization translates priorities and coverage goals into a practical verification plan.

The main value is the ability to identify what deserves attention, explain why it matters, compare alternatives, and coordinate a response before an issue becomes urgent. Technical calculations remain available for analytical review while operational questions remain accessible to non-modeling users.

The next measure of success is whether decisions improve in practice. A disciplined pilot should connect recommendations with approved actions and observed results in service, inventory, counting effort, and storage use.
